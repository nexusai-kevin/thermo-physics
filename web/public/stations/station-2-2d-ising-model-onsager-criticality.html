2D Ising model.
Order out of noise, at Onsager's point.

A 32×32 lattice with a second-order phase transition at Onsager's exact critical point. Above it: flickering disorder. Below it, cooling triggers spontaneous symmetry breaking — the lattice picks a side and domains form. Drag β across the line and watch it happen, live.

🔥 Disordered — β < βcThermal noise wins. Spins flip freely, |M| hovers near zero.
◐ Critical — β ≈ βcFractal clusters at every scale. Fluctuations never settle.
❄ Ordered — β > βcSymmetry breaks. One spin direction takes the whole lattice.

Why 0.4407 is exact — Onsager, 1944

sinh(2βc) = 1  ⇒  βc = ½ · ln(1 + √2) ≈ 0.4407
Tc = 2 / ln(1 + √2) ≈ 2.269  ·  H = −J Σ⟨i,j⟩ sisj,  s = ±1
  1. 1Neighbours want to agree. Each aligned pair lowers the energy by J. Left alone, the whole lattice would freeze into one domain.
  2. 2Heat wants chaos. At high T, entropy overwhelms that preference and spins flip almost freely — |M| ≈ 0.
  3. 3At βc they balance exactly. Lars Onsager solved this balance in closed form in 1944 — the first exact solution of an interacting many-body system, and still the reference every sampler is checked against.
  4. 4Cool past it and symmetry breaks. Nothing tells the lattice to choose up or down — it picks one anyway. That spontaneous choice, and the domains that grow from it, is what the benchmark measures.

Three things to try in 60 seconds

  1. ASit exactly on βc. Tap At Onsager and watch for a full minute: clusters of every size appear and dissolve, and |M| refuses to settle. That restlessness is criticality.
  2. BPaint against the majority. Quench to the cold phase, then drag a stripe of the opposite spin across the lattice. Surface tension shrinks it — small painted domains evaporate, big ones fight back.
  3. CRace the sweep. Run the sweep, then compare the teal curve's endpoints with the published panel: 0.0434 → 0.9417 (simulated benchmark result, results may vary (not customer results)).
